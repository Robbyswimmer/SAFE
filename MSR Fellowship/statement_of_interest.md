## Motivation: Rethinking Multimodal Architecture Design

During my tenure as a Machine Learning Engineer at Microsoft on the People Skills team, I confronted a fundamental limitation in how multimodal systems process non-textual information. While working on Microsoft CoPilot, I developed retrieval-augmented generation architectures that synthesized information across millions of entries in the M365 knowledge graph. This experience revealed a critical architectural challenge: contemporary multimodal LLMs treat all modalities through the lens of text tokenization, forcing visual, auditory, and other sensory inputs into representations designed for discrete linguistic units. This tokenization-centric paradigm fails to capture the unique structural and relational properties inherent to different modalities—a limitation that becomes acute in production systems serving diverse global populations where knowledge exists in video, speech, and embodied interaction.

My research directly addresses this architectural challenge by developing foundational approaches that move beyond tokenization toward modality-aware representations. Rather than forcing all sensory inputs through text-based tokenization pipelines, I propose architectures where each modality is processed through specialized, structurally-aligned pathways that preserve its inherent characteristics—continuous temporal dynamics for audio, spatial hierarchies for vision, sequential structure for text. Inspired by human cognition, where distinct cortical regions process different sensory modalities before integration, my framework employs modular components that respect modality-specific properties while enabling flexible cross-modal reasoning.

This work synthesizes my production experience at Microsoft—where I improved inference accuracy from 50% to 94% while reducing errors by 70% across 50 million inferences—with rigorous academic research on multimodal fusion and parameter-efficient architectures. The framework represents a paradigm shift: rather than viewing multimodal integration as extending text tokenization, I treat it as composing specialized modality encoders with adaptive fusion mechanisms that enable natural cross-modal understanding. Critically, this approach maintains safety guarantees on existing capabilities, ensuring that adding new modalities does not degrade performance on tasks the model already handles well—a requirement essential for production deployment and inclusive technology development.

## Technical Approach: Modality-Aware Architectures Beyond Tokenization

The core innovation of my research lies in developing foundational architectures that respect each modality's inherent structure rather than forcing all inputs through text-centric representations. This approach directly addresses the limitations of tokenization-based multimodal systems, where the discrete, sequential nature of text tokens poorly matches the continuous, hierarchical, or temporal structure of other modalities.

**Modality-Specific Processing Pathways**
My framework employs specialized encoders that preserve modality-specific characteristics. For audio, I use frozen CLAP or Whisper encoders that capture temporal dynamics and acoustic patterns in their native continuous representation space. For vision, CLIP-based encoders maintain spatial hierarchies and object relationships. Rather than immediately tokenizing these representations, the architecture processes them through modality-aware projectors that map features into a shared semantic space while preserving structural information. This design philosophy—inspired by human sensory processing where distinct cortical regions specialize in different modalities—enables richer, more natural multimodal understanding than tokenization-based approaches.

**Adaptive Cross-Modal Fusion**
The fusion mechanism moves beyond simple concatenation or early fusion strategies. I employ parameter-efficient LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) layers [4,5] to enable flexible attention across modalities at multiple depths within the language model. This interleaved attention design allows the system to dynamically determine how and when to integrate information from different modalities based on the task requirements. For example, in audio-visual understanding tasks, the model can attend to audio features when processing temporal descriptions while primarily using visual features for spatial reasoning. This adaptive integration mirrors human cognitive flexibility in weighting different sensory inputs based on context.

Critically, the LoRA-based approach achieves parameter efficiency—training only 1-2% of the full model parameters—while maintaining strong generalization. This efficiency is essential for scaling to larger foundation models and for practical deployment in resource-constrained environments, supporting the goal of inclusive technology accessible to the global majority.

**Safety-Preserving Training and Gated Integration**
A critical challenge in multimodal architecture design is ensuring that adding new modalities does not degrade existing capabilities—what the continual learning literature terms catastrophic forgetting [1,2]. My framework addresses this through a gated bypass mechanism that provides architectural safety guarantees. The system can seamlessly operate in either single-modality mode (gate closed, preserving original model behavior) or multimodal mode (gate open, enabling cross-modal reasoning). This design ensures mathematical equivalence to the base model when new modalities are disabled, providing a hard guarantee against regression.

The training methodology reinforces these architectural guarantees through multi-objective optimization that balances new capability acquisition with existing performance retention. I employ Fisher Information Matrix regularization [6] to identify and protect parameters critical to base model performance, combined with knowledge distillation to maintain output distributions on original tasks. This creates a "safe learning corridor" where the model can acquire new multimodal capabilities without disrupting existing knowledge—essential for both production deployment and for enabling inclusive technologies that must maintain reliability across diverse user populations.

**Preliminary Results and Validation**
Initial experiments demonstrate the viability of this approach. Training on AudioCaps, a benchmark audio captioning dataset, I achieved a CIDEr score of 127.2—significantly exceeding baseline performance—while maintaining vision-language performance within 0.5% of the original model. Critically, when the audio gate is disabled, the model exhibits identical behavior to the base LLaVA model, confirming zero regression at the architectural level. These results validate that the gated fusion architecture can successfully integrate new modalities without compromising existing capabilities.

The training process revealed important insights about the dynamics of modality addition. Early experiments encountered the "comma loop of death"—a failure mode where the model generates repetitive, meaningless outputs when audio embeddings are out-of-distribution relative to the language model's expected input space [7]. Solving this required careful calibration of the audio projector's output normalization to match the magnitude distribution of the language model's internal representations. This technical challenge underscores the subtlety of modality fusion: it is insufficient to simply connect encoders; the feature spaces must be carefully aligned to ensure stable gradient flow and coherent generation.

## Collaboration Vision and Expected Impact

This research aligns directly with the "Beyond Tokenization: Foundational Approaches to Multimodal LLMs" challenge led by Principal Investigators Vineeth N Balasubramanian and Tanuja Ganu at Microsoft Research India, along with collaborators Mercy Ranjit, Neeraj Kayal, and Ogbemi Ekwejunor-Etchie. My work on modality-aware architectures, parameter-efficient fusion, and safety-preserving integration directly addresses the challenge's core objectives of moving beyond tokenization toward structurally-aligned, cognitively-inspired multimodal systems. I envision collaboration in several high-impact directions:

**Foundational Architecture Design Beyond Tokenization**
The challenge seeks to explore representations that are "structurally and functionally aligned with each modality's characteristics"—precisely the focus of my framework. Collaboration with Dr. Balasubramanian (whose expertise in computer vision and machine learning) and Dr. Ganu (with her background in AI systems) would enable rigorous investigation of how different architectural choices—projector designs, fusion strategies, attention mechanisms—affect the preservation of modality-specific structure. We could systematically evaluate how well different approaches capture temporal dynamics in audio, spatial hierarchies in vision, and sequential patterns in text, developing principled guidelines for modality-aware architecture design.

Beyond the audio-visual integration I have explored, collaboration could extend the framework to additional modalities critical for inclusive technology and embodied AI: depth sensing for robotics, tactile feedback for human-computer interaction, or multimodal inputs from resource-constrained devices serving the global majority. Each modality presents unique structural challenges, making this a rich research area with broad applicability across the domains the challenge targets—robotics, copilots, and embodied AI systems.

**Interleaved Attention and Cognitive-Inspired Integration**
The challenge explicitly seeks architectures that "interleave attention across modalities" inspired by human cognitive processing. My current fusion approach employs LoRA-based cross-attention at multiple depths within the language model, but systematic exploration of attention patterns—when to fuse, at which layers, with what mechanisms—remains an open research question. Collaboration with the MSR India team could investigate cognitively-inspired fusion strategies: Do certain modality combinations benefit from early fusion (mimicking primary sensory cortex integration) versus late fusion (analogous to higher-level semantic processing)? How should attention weights adapt based on task requirements and input characteristics? Insights from Dr. Balasubramanian's computer vision work and Dr. Ganu's AI systems expertise would be invaluable in designing and evaluating these architectures.

**Parameter and Sample Efficiency for Inclusive Technology**
The challenge emphasizes efficiency as a core objective—essential for deploying multimodal systems that serve the global majority, where computational resources may be constrained and large labeled datasets scarce. My LoRA-based approach achieves strong parameter efficiency (1-2% trainable parameters), but collaboration could push further: investigating ultra-low-rank adaptations, sparse attention patterns, or modular components that can be selectively activated based on available resources. Sample efficiency is equally critical—can we leverage the structural priors inherent in modality-specific encoders to achieve few-shot or zero-shot generalization to new tasks? This direction connects to Dr. Kayal's theoretical expertise and could yield both practical deployment benefits and fundamental insights into multimodal learning dynamics.

**Evaluation Benchmarks and Multimodal Reasoning Pipelines**
The challenge aims to produce "evaluation benchmarks and multimodal reasoning pipelines applicable to domains such as robotics, copilots, and embodied AI systems." My work has focused on audio captioning (AudioCaps) and vision-language tasks (VQA), but comprehensive evaluation requires benchmarks that truly test cross-modal reasoning—not just processing each modality independently. Collaboration could develop novel evaluation paradigms: tasks requiring temporal alignment between audio and visual streams, reasoning about causal relationships across modalities, or handling missing/corrupted modality inputs gracefully. These benchmarks would drive the field toward genuinely multimodal architectures rather than ensembles of single-modality systems.

**Applications to Robotics and Embodied AI**
The framework's modularity and efficiency make it particularly well-suited for embodied AI applications, where agents must integrate diverse sensory inputs (vision, audio, proprioception, tactile) in real-time under computational constraints. Collaboration with Dr. Ranjit and the MSR India team could explore applications to robotic systems that must understand multimodal instructions (spoken commands + visual demonstrations), navigate using audiovisual cues, or interact with humans through natural multimodal communication. These applications would validate the framework's practical utility while contributing to the challenge's broader goal of advancing embodied AI systems.

**Expected Impact: Advancing Foundational Multimodal AI Research**
This collaboration has the potential to produce foundational contributions that reshape how the research community approaches multimodal LLM architecture design. Moving beyond tokenization is not merely a technical refinement—it represents a philosophical shift in how we think about modality integration. By developing architectures that respect each modality's inherent structure, we can create systems that more naturally mirror human multimodal cognition, potentially unlocking richer cross-modal reasoning capabilities.

The challenge's emphasis on inclusive technology resonates deeply with my motivations. Much of the world's knowledge exists in non-textual forms—oral traditions preserved in speech, crafts demonstrated visually, cultural practices embodied in action. Multimodal systems that can truly understand these diverse forms of knowledge representation are essential for ensuring AI benefits all of humanity, not just text-literate populations in resource-rich environments. The parameter and sample efficiency enabled by modality-aware architectures directly supports this vision, making advanced multimodal AI accessible even in resource-constrained settings.

Academically, publications at top-tier venues (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL, CVPR) would disseminate prototype architectures, evaluation benchmarks, and design principles that could influence the broader research community. Open-source implementations and comprehensive documentation would lower barriers to entry, enabling other researchers to build on this foundation. Beyond individual papers, the collaboration could establish multimodal architecture design as a mature subfield with principled methodologies, standardized evaluation protocols, and theoretical foundations.

For Microsoft and the broader industry, this research offers practical pathways to safer, more efficient multimodal systems. The framework's applicability to CoPilot (integrating audio for meeting understanding), Azure AI (offering modality addition as a service), accessibility tools (adding modalities for diverse user needs), and robotics applications demonstrates immediate deployment potential.
